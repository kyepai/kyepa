Life Experience Designer

Your mind, mapped. Your path, designed. Your moment, produced.

"Life Experience Designer" is a compound, on purpose. Three fields sit inside it: Cognitive Behavioural Therapy, cross-functional User Experience Design, and my original background in Artistic Production Assistance. CBT is the structural half — reading how thoughts, habits, and behaviours reinforce each other, and finding the small intervention that creates real, lasting change. UX Design is the cross-functional half — designing around how a person actually moves through a system, not how you'd like them to. And underneath both sits Artistic Production Assistance: years spent making sure what an audience experiences in a room lands exactly as intended, down to the smallest detail.

Put those three together and you get someone who doesn't hand you a program. I design the experience around you — the movement practice, the training structure, the environment — the way a good production is designed: built around what needs to land, and built around who's actually standing in the room.

So when you ask what a Life Experience Designer does: I build the structure — cognitive, physical, experiential — for you to move through your own life with more intention, less friction, and a great deal more honesty about what you're actually building it for.

Reading List

HWT

How the World Thinks— Julian Baggini

Baggini treats philosophical traditions from Japan, India, China, the Muslim world, Africa and Australia with the same seriousness as anything out of Athens. His argument: there's no single correct architecture for reasoning about a good life, only different, internally coherent ones. I design the same way — not by installing my framework in you, but by understanding the one you're already running on.

TES

The Ethical Slut— Dossie Easton & Janet W. Hardy

Its real subject is this: real change happens inside radical honesty. Not the performative kind. The kind where you say the true thing over the tidy one, and take full ownership of what you actually want. That's the standard I hold myself to, and the one I ask of anyone I work with.

SPR

Sprint— Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky & Braden Kowitz

Five days, one prototype, no meetings that go nowhere. It's the process I lean on whenever something needs to move from talking to testing fast — clear the calendar, get in a room, build something real.

DOET

The Design of Everyday Things— Don Norman

Norman's rule sits underneath every session I run: when someone struggles to use a thing, the fault is rarely theirs. Good design reads as obvious in hindsight — that's the standard I hold my own structures to.

AUFN

A Universe from Nothing— Lawrence M. Krauss

Krauss makes the case that even nothing has structure, rules, potential. I keep it close as a reminder that starting from zero — a blank practice, a client with no plan — isn't starting from nothing at all.

Movement is Life

Nearly two decades in, if you ask what I bring into a room, it isn't showmanship — it's a deep, intuitive mastery of body control and articulation, matched with an urgency about movement that's been embedded in me since long before I had language for it.

Before movement, there was mathematics. Architecture, too — I had genuine ambitions of teaching either; structure and precision were always where my mind wanted to live. But somewhere in the early 2000s, the drive to move overtook the drive to explain how things were built. Music videos were still a real cultural force back then, well before our media diet fractured into the disjointed way we consume things now — and that was the world that pulled me in.

My actual start in dance, though, was less a decision than a shove: thrown into the deep end of the pool, on Sint Maarten, early 2000s. "Dance," even as a hobby, wasn't the easiest look for a guy to wear at the time — not with Jamaica sitting right next door, its musical influence already seeped well beyond the Caribbean Sea and merged into the region's wider lexicon and attitudes. I still love and enjoy Dancehall and Reggaeton today for what they are. I'll also say plainly that both unfortunately carry lyrics that are largely homophobic and misogynistic — and being one of the few boys who dared to dance at all, in that climate, came with few advantages at the time. Most of those advantages only become recognizable in hindsight.

One of them, unglamorous as it sounds: ratio. Boys were so outnumbered that if you showed even a bit of talent, you'd be thrust straight into the advanced classes and carried along on sheer encouragement until you caught up to where you were "supposed" to be. That ratio didn't stay behind on the island. It followed me into my university years.

Being a Dutch national, moving to the Netherlands to pursue a bachelor's degree after finishing HAVO was relatively standard procedure. Many students made that crossing with one shoe left at home, so to speak. I never packed that way — I saw it as my great escape, my chance to venture out into the wider world where I belonged, all the freedom I'd imagined finally within reach.

The program itself didn't quite live up to the fantasy I'd built around it. I went to two universities, and the ratio that shaped my island years followed me straight into the first one: my inaugural class at Fontys Dance Academy, in 2007/08, had an anomalous number of young men sign up. I didn't see out the second year there — before it closed, I'd decided to change schools and continue my studies in Rotterdam, at Codarts Dance Academy, 2009/10. It was a different animal entirely: much less homie, much less cozy, run more like an industry machine than a school. Given the notoriety that came with being a Codarts alumnus, it was an easy trade to make.

Before the close of 2010, I'd met Nanine Linning. Today she's the Artistic Leader of Scapino Ballet in Rotterdam; back then, she had exactly one year under her belt as Company Director of the city theater in Osnabrück, Germany. I joined her company as an apprentice, trading my final year of taking classes as a student for a full season's worth of real workfield and stage experience instead. I graduated in 2011, the same year the company changed host cities — most of us moved to Heidelberg with it, and I stayed an integral member of the company until 2016.

By the point I eventually moved to Berlin, I'd already been living in Germany for six years. I'd never once enrolled in a language or integration course — and yet, almost by osmosis, I'd absorbed the language to a degree that surprised even me. Enough that walking away from it felt like it would have been a pity, throwing all of that away. Six years, one country, zero classes, a language I could actually live inside of — quietly, that was its own kind of highlight, and the note this chapter closes on before Berlin properly begins.

ALWAYS on BEAT and in the POCKET

'Style one's self as best as possible to the occasion.'

I'd been living by that line for years before I realized it was also the answer to a question nobody had asked me yet: what happens to a dancer's discipline once the stages stop calling and the clubs start.

Club Athleten found me the way most good things in Berlin do — sideways, through someone who knew someone, at an hour when the distinction between "workout" and "night out" had already stopped meaning anything. Their whole premise is the wall I'd spent a decade quietly dismantling in my own life: fitness on one side, club culture on the other, never to touch. Club Athleten touches them constantly, on purpose. Classes built on boxing combinations and powerband work, run inside actual Berlin venues, with a DJ playing an honest-to-god live set instead of a Spotify playlist piped through gym speakers. Sweat first, then the bar. "Sassy workouts, sweaty DJ sets," as they put it — and I remember thinking, well, that's just Tuesday.

What pulled me in wasn't the novelty. It was the recognition. Years of styling myself for a door selection had taught me things about a room — how to read its energy, how to move a body through it, how to make forty strangers feel like they'd been let into something rather than sold something — that translate directly onto a training floor. Club Athleten wasn't asking me to leave the dancer at the door. They wanted the choreographer's eye, the DJ-literate ear, the years of floor time, brought inside as instruction rather than performance. Joining their team wasn't a career pivot so much as a rename: I'd been doing some version of this — reading the room, cueing the drop, getting a group of strangers to move as one thing — since the first company I ever joined. It just took a club with a sound system and a squat rack to give it a job title.

Techno Reclamation

Long before it was a sound you needed the right posture to get past a bouncer for, techno was Black and it was American — three teenagers from Belleville, Michigan, splicing Parliament-Funkadelic's cosmic slop together with Kraftwerk's precision, because a synthesizer, unlike a factory job, doesn't care what you look like when it hires you. Juan Atkins, Derrick May, Kevin Saunderson — the Belleville Three — built the blueprint in Detroit's Black middle class, feeding it late at night to The Electrifying Mojo's radio show, the DJ whose refusal to sort records by genre or race is half the reason Kraftwerk and Parliament ever ended up in the same mix to begin with. What came out of it was what one writer called "a deprived sound trying to get out" — which is Afrofuturism doing exactly what it always does: taking the machine that was supposed to replace you and making it sing on your terms instead. And it isn't a distant cousin to what I grew up around — it's the same family. It's the current Paul Gilroy named the Black Atlantic: the same one that carried Jamaica's soundsystem culture across the sea to Sint Maarten's doorstep is the one that carried funk and soul into Detroit's basements and came back out the other side as a four-on-the-floor kick drum.

It's also traceable, almost embarrassingly precisely: a 1988 Virgin Records compilation called Techno! The New Dance Sound of Detroit is the reason the genre has that name at all, and it's the record that physically carried this sound across the Atlantic into England, Belgium, and Germany — handing European producers the blueprint direct from the source.

Somewhere between Detroit and the club I had to dress correctly for, that origin got quietly edited out of the story. The scholar Alexander Weheliye has documented this in detail: West Berlin had Black club culture — funk, R&B, house — running through it well before 1989, and around 1992, as the city's producers built the harder, more "metronomic" sound that would become internationally synonymous with Berlin, that scene got written out of the story. It happened alongside, not apart from, the violence people of color faced during German reunification — violence the official narrative still treats as an exception rather than as part of how the city rebuilt its own myth. Blackness stayed welcome in that myth only from a safe transatlantic distance — celebrated in Detroit's producers, absent from Berlin's own dancefloors. There's a whole movement now doing the work of putting that authorship back where it belongs: Make Techno Black Again, built around the theorist and producer DeForrest Brown Jr., is the bluntest, most literal name for it — arguing that the genres funding the modern music industry were, plainly, taken from Black people, and that fixing it takes more than a credit line.

I didn't coin "Techno Reclamation" as an academic position. I coined it standing in a Berlin queue, aware that the anonymity and clout I was chasing were built, underneath everything, on a lineage that already belonged to someone who looked like me. Anonymity and personal branding turned out to be two ends of the same rope: the more I disappeared into Berlin's version of the culture, the more I understood I was actually looking for a way back to where it started — which, for me, isn't Detroit. It's Sint Maarten, and the youth there who've never been told that the music filling out a Berlin warehouse at 4am has their own grandparents' fingerprints on it. That's the branding, stripped down to its actual ambition: not "dancer who lived in Berlin," but someone carrying the reclamation home, teaching the technique with the history still attached.

That's not an argument I make from the sidelines — it's a practice I show up for. Since at least September 2025 I've been part of Das Techno Team, the Berlin collective behind Resonanz: The Movement of Sound — a dance workshop set to live electronic music. My credit on it is "Impulses," the movement layer, standing in the same production credit block as Light and Sound, because Resonanz treats the body moving through a room as its own instrument, not an accessory to the ones plugged in. What started as Berlin editions became an international premiere at Slakthuset in Stockholm, then a Copenhagen edition, with my name on the lineup poster alongside John Fick, Ritual Cycles, Eitan Shinder, and Molly Lollen — and, less formally but no less sincerely, the team's own "Head of Upper Body Operations." Techno Reclamation isn't a position I hold at a remove from the culture. It's the room I'm standing in most weekends, doing the actual work of putting the body back at the center of a sound that, for a while, tried to forget it ever had one.
