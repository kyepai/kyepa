### [bullet 4 — crossover into fitness instruction]

'Style one's self as best as possible to the occasion.'

I'd been living by that line for years before I realized it was also the answer to a question nobody had asked me yet: what happens to a dancer's discipline once the stages stop calling and the clubs start.

Club Athleten found me the way most good things in Berlin do — sideways, through someone who knew someone, at an hour when the distinction between "workout" and "night out" had already stopped meaning anything. Their whole premise is the wall I'd spent a decade quietly dismantling in my own life: fitness on one side, club culture on the other, never to touch. Club Athleten touches them constantly, on purpose. Classes built on boxing combinations and powerband work, run inside actual Berlin venues, with a DJ playing an honest-to-god live set instead of a Spotify playlist piped through gym speakers. Sweat first, then the bar. "Sassy workouts, sweaty DJ sets," as they put it — and I remember thinking, well, that's just Tuesday.

What pulled me in wasn't the novelty. It was the recognition. Years of styling myself for a door selection had taught me things about a room — how to read its energy, how to move a body through it, how to make forty strangers feel like they'd been let into something rather than sold something — that translate directly onto a training floor. Club Athleten wasn't asking me to leave the dancer at the door. They wanted the choreographer's eye, the DJ-literate ear, the years of floor time, brought inside as instruction rather than performance. Joining their team wasn't a career pivot so much as a rename: I'd been doing some version of this — reading the room, cueing the drop, getting a group of strangers to move as one thing — since the first company I ever joined. It just took a club with a sound system and a squat rack to give it a job title.

### [bullet 5 — the afro-centric roots of electronic dance music]

Here's the part of the survival guide nobody puts on the laminated card they hand you at the door.

Long before it was a sound you needed the right posture to get past a bouncer for, techno was Black and it was American — three teenagers from Belleville, Michigan, splicing Parliament-Funkadelic's cosmic slop together with Kraftwerk's precision, because a synthesizer, unlike a factory job, doesn't care what you look like when it hires you. Juan Atkins, Derrick May, Kevin Saunderson — the Belleville Three — built the blueprint in Detroit's Black middle class out of what one writer called "a deprived sound trying to get out." That's Afrofuturism doing exactly what it always does: taking the machine that was supposed to replace you and making it sing on your terms instead. And it isn't a distant cousin to what I grew up around — it's the same family. The same Black Atlantic current that carried Jamaica's soundsystem culture across the sea to Sint Maarten's doorstep is the one that carried funk and soul into Detroit's basements and came back out the other side as a four-on-the-floor kick drum.

Somewhere between Detroit and the club I had to dress correctly for, that origin got quietly edited out of the story. Berlin's own techno history has a well-documented habit of scrubbing the funk and the swing out of the sound right around reunification, and a harder habit still of celebrating Black American producers from a safe transatlantic distance while keeping Black Germans off its own dancefloors — Blackness welcome, so long as it stayed foreign. There's a whole movement now — Make Techno Black Again is the bluntest, most literal name for it — doing the work of putting that authorship back where it belongs.

I didn't coin "Techno Reclamation" as an academic position. I coined it standing in a Berlin queue, aware that the anonymity and clout I was chasing were built, underneath everything, on a lineage that already belonged to someone who looked like me. Anonymity and personal branding turned out to be two ends of the same rope: the more I disappeared into Berlin's version of the culture, the more I understood I was actually looking for a way back to where it started — which, for me, isn't Detroit. It's Sint Maarten, and the youth there who've never been told that the music filling out a Berlin warehouse at 4am has their own grandparents' fingerprints on it. That's the branding, stripped down to its actual ambition: not "dancer who lived in Berlin," but someone carrying the reclamation home, teaching the technique with the history still attached.
